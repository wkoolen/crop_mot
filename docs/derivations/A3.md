# Model
## Model assumptions


**Measurement model:**
$$
\begin{aligned}
&\begin{aligned}
p(Z \mid x) & =\sum_{\theta=0}^m p(Z, \theta \mid x) \\
& =\left[\left(1-P^{\mathrm{D}}(x)\right)+P^{\mathrm{D}}(x) \sum_{\theta=1}^m \frac{g_k\left(z^\theta \mid x\right)}{\lambda_c\left(z^\theta\right)}\right] \frac{\exp \left(-\bar{\lambda}_c\right)}{m!} \prod_{i=1}^m \lambda_c\left(z^i\right) .
\end{aligned}
\end{aligned}
$$

**Posterior Density:**
$$
\begin{aligned}
p(x \mid Z) & \propto p(x) p(Z \mid x) \\
& \propto p(x)\left[\left(1-P^{\mathrm{D}}(x)\right)+P^{\mathrm{D}}(x) \sum_{\theta=1}^m \frac{g\left(z^\theta \mid x\right)}{\lambda_c\left(z^\theta\right)}\right] .
\end{aligned}
$$

Then the desired form:
$$p(x|Z)=\sum\limits_{\theta=0}^m w_{\theta}p_\theta (x)$$
where:$$\begin{cases}{w}_\theta 
\quad & \text{is a pmf } (Pr[\theta|Z]) \text{ non-negative and sums to 1}\\ p_\theta(x) & \text{is a pdf } (p(x|\theta,Z)) \text{ non-negative and integrates to 1}\end{cases}$$
# Normalization


## Problem formulation

Omitting indices such that it gives the incentive of a general solution, we can consider a probability density function:
$$p(x)\propto g(x)=\sum\limits_{\theta=0}^mg_\theta(x),$$
where $g_0(x),\dots,g_m(x)$ are *non-negative functions* that have with finite (*closed integrals*), i.e.:
$$0<\int g_i(x)dx<\infty$$
> **[figure omitted in mirror: non-negative and closed.png - see the vault note]**
Where we would like to express $p(x)$ as a mixture of *pdfs*, i.e.: $$p(x)=\sum\limits^{m}_{\theta=0}w_\theta p_\theta(x)$$
> **[figure omitted in mirror: pdf_mixture.png - see the vault note]**
The image shows the mixture of $p(x)$ normalized with weights $w_0, w_1$ such that the solution (red curve) integrates to one.

## Normalizing a function *(theory)*

Consider for a function $p(x)$ being proportional to $g(x)$ by some constant $c$:
$$p(x)=cg(x)?$$
Given that $p(x)$ is a **pdf** such that:
$$\int p(x)dx=c\int g(x)dx=1 \Rightarrow c=\frac{1}{\int g(x)dx}$$

> [!Note] Remember: Normalizing a density
> If $p(x)$ is proportional to $g(x)$, then$$p(x)=\frac{g(x)}{\int g(x')dx'}$$
> 
> > **[figure omitted in mirror: Normalizing a density.png - see the vault note]**

## Factorizing $g_\theta (x)$ 

Introducing the following set, $\tilde w_\theta$: $$\begin{cases}\tilde w_\theta =\int g_\theta (x)dx \\ p_\theta (x)= \frac{g_\theta (x)}{\tilde w_\theta}
\end{cases}$$we get the factorization-relation $g_\theta (x)=\tilde w_\theta p_\theta(x)$ with $p_\theta(x)$ being a *pdf*. Making use of the relationship constructed above we can plug in back $p_\theta (x)$, i.e.
$$g_\theta (x) =\frac{\tilde w_\theta g_\theta (x)}{\tilde w_\theta} =g_\theta(x)$$
Then it follows that $$p(x)\propto g(x)=\sum\limits^m_{\theta=0}\tilde w_\theta p_\theta (x)$$> **[figure omitted in mirror: factorizing p_theta.png - see the vault note]**

## Normalizing the mixture

We now have two relations:
$$\begin{cases}p(x)\propto g(x) \Rightarrow p(x)=\dfrac{g(x)}{\int g(x')dx'} \\ g(x)=\sum\limits^m_{\theta=0}g_\theta(x)=\sum\limits^m_{\theta=0}\tilde w_\theta p_\theta(x)\end{cases}$$
So the only thing missing is the normalizing constant $\int g(x)dx$.

**Step 1: integral of $g(x)$.** The integral of a sum is the sum of the integrals, and each $g_\theta$ integrates to $\tilde w_\theta$ by definition:
$$\int g(x)dx=\sum\limits^m_{\theta=0}\int g_\theta(x)dx=\sum\limits^m_{\theta=0}\tilde w_\theta$$
i.e. the total "mass" of $g$ is just the sum of the unnormalized weights.

**Step 2: plug into the normalization.**
$$p(x)=\frac{g(x)}{\int g(x')dx'}=\frac{\sum\limits^m_{\theta=0}\tilde w_\theta p_\theta(x)}{\sum\limits^m_{i=0}\tilde w_i}$$
(index $i$ in the denominator so it does not clash with $\theta$ in the numerator.)

**Step 3: define normalized weights.** The denominator is a constant, so it can be pulled inside the sum:
$$w_\theta=\frac{\tilde w_\theta}{\sum\limits^m_{i=0}\tilde w_i} \quad\Rightarrow\quad p(x)=\sum\limits^m_{\theta=0}w_\theta p_\theta(x)$$

**Check that this is the desired form:**
- $w_\theta\geq 0$ since every $\tilde w_\theta>0$, and $\sum_\theta w_\theta=\frac{\sum_\theta\tilde w_\theta}{\sum_i\tilde w_i}=1$, so $w_\theta$ is a pmf.
- $p_\theta(x)$ is a pdf by construction (factorization step).
- So $\int p(x)dx=\sum_\theta w_\theta\int p_\theta(x)dx=\sum_\theta w_\theta=1$.

**Thought process:** normalizing the full mixture never requires integrating $g(x)$ as a whole. We only need the integral of each component separately ($\tilde w_\theta$), then rescale the weights so they sum to one. The shapes $p_\theta(x)$ stay untouched, only the weights change.

**Example:** $g(x)=3p_0(x)+2p_1(x)$, so $\tilde w_0=3$, $\tilde w_1=2$.
$$\int g(x)dx=3+2=5 \quad\Rightarrow\quad w_0=\tfrac{3}{5}=0.6,\quad w_1=\tfrac{2}{5}=0.4$$
$$p(x)=0.6\,p_0(x)+0.4\,p_1(x)$$
The shape of $p(x)$ (red) is the same as $g(x)$ (black), only scaled down by a factor 5 so the area becomes 1.

> [!Note] Remember: Normalizing a mixture
> If $p(x)\propto\sum_\theta\tilde w_\theta p_\theta(x)$ with $p_\theta$ pdfs, then $$p(x)=\sum_\theta w_\theta p_\theta(x),\qquad w_\theta=\frac{\tilde w_\theta}{\sum_i\tilde w_i}$$
