## Model assumptions

Problem formulation and familiarization with the standard bayes filter and its connection to the kalman filter. Formulating the discrete dynamics system. The classical discrete-time additive noise formulation as:
$$x_k=f_{k-1}(x_{k-1})+v_{k-1}$$
$$z_k=h_k(x_k)+w_k$$
which are respectively the *dynamics equation* and the *measurement equation*. where $k$ is the discrete time index that belongs to the time instance $t_k$. and $x_k\in\mathcal{X}$ is *the complete specification of the state of a dynamic system*. Typically for the case $z_k\in\mathcal{Z}$ where both complete specifications are a super set or equal to $\mathcal{R}^{n_x}$ and $\mathcal{R}^{n_z}$ respectively for the state and measurement where partial observation is when $n_z< n_x$.

In the aforementioned formulation it is typically assumed that functions $f_k$, $h_k$ and probability distributions for noise, are known.

Then the formulation define 2 probability functions:

**The transitional density:** (Distribution of the motion model)
$$\pi(x_k|x_{k-1})=p_v(x_k-f_{k-1}(x_{k-1}))$$
**Likelihood function:** (Distribution of the measurement model)
$$g_k(z_k|x_k)=p_w(z_k-h_k(x_k))$$
The classical problem given these formulations is to compute recursively the posterior PDF of the state, $p_{k|k}(x_k|z_{1:k})$ at $k$. The solution to this problem is presented in two steps.

**Posterior PDF at $k-1$:**
$$p_{k-1|k-1}(x_{k-1}|z_{1:k-1})$$
**Prediction density at $k$ via Chapman Kolmogorov:**
$$p_{k|k-1}(x_k|z_{1:k-1})=\int \pi_{k|k-1}(x_k|x')p(x'|z_{1:k-1})dx'$$
**Update $p(x_k|z_{1:k-1})$ using $z_k$**
$$p_{k|k}(x_{k}|z_{1:k})=\frac{g_k(z_k|x_k)p_{k|k-1}(x_k|z_{1:k-1})}{\int g_k(z_k|x)p_{k|k-1}(x|z_{1:k-1})dx}$$
Using the posterior $p(x_k|z_{1:k-1})$ you can compute a point estimate of the state $\hat{x}_k$ using the mean and confidence of the posterior and interval respectively.

The closed form solution to the prediction and update step is computed easily in some specific cases. One specifically is when, $f_k$ and $h_k$ are linear and $p_v$, $p_w$ and $p_0$ are Gaussian, the solution breaks down to the Kalman Filter.

**Analytical approximations:**
- (Extended/Unscented) Kalman Filter
	- the EKF _forces_ property 1 back by replacing ff f with its Jacobian, while the UKF doesn't linearize at all — it pushes sample points through the true $f$, computes the resulting mean and covariance, and then refits a Gaussian to them. Both end by projecting back onto the Gaussian family; they just disagree about how to get the moments.
- Grid-based methods
- Gaussian sum filters ($p(x_k|z_{1:k-1})$ is approximated by a Gaussian mixture)
	- What if $p_{k-1\mid k-1}$ is not gaussian, Chapman Kolmogorov still holds. but now multiple hypotheses have to be tracked. Now you carry forth: ($w_1,\hat{x}_1,P_1$) and ($w_2,\hat{x}_2,P_2$) now the problem consists of 6 numbers instead of just a state and a probability.
- Particle filters

### Enumerated state space (framework step 2)

Per Derivation framework, the branches have to be listed before Bayes' rule can be written. For the classical Kalman case the list has **one entry**:

$$X=\{x\},\qquad p_D=1,\qquad \lambda_{\text{FA}}=0,\qquad r=1$$

The object exists, it is always detected, the sensor never invents a reading, and the one measurement received is the one it produced. That is the whole enumeration — and it is the reason the classical filter looks so much simpler than everything downstream of it. The three new scalars of the notation dictionary are all pinned to their degenerate values here.

## Branches

| Branch                                      | Prior                              | Likelihood of observed data |
| ------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | --------------------------- |
| object at $x$, detected, $z_k$ came from it | $p_{k\mid k-1}(x_k\mid z_{1:k-1})$ | $g_k(z_k\mid x_k)$          |
|                                             |                                    |                             |

One row. **This is why the normaliser is invisible in the control-course version of the filter:** with a single branch the denominator is a constant that divides out of the density and never has to be looked at. It only becomes an object worth naming once there is a second row to compare against — which is exactly what A2 (A2 Bernoulli existence update) and A4 do by re-inflating $p_D$, $\lambda_{\text{FA}}$ and $r$ off their degenerate values.

#### Explicit derivation and reading Chapman Kolmogorov equation

The main reason for this script is to be able to fluently understand the integral as reading it like a regular *gain* instead of this complex formulation.

**The identity, strictly.** Chapman–Kolmogorov composes two transition kernels into one:

$$p(x_k\mid x_{k-2})=\int \underbrace{\pi(x_k\mid x_{k-1})}_{\text{second hop}}\;\underbrace{\pi(x_{k-1}\mid x_{k-2})}_{\text{first hop}}\;dx_{k-1}$$

Read as: **multiply the transition densities along a path, integrate over the intermediate state.** The factors multiplied are the *transitions*, not the states — that distinction is the whole content of the identity. Over $n$ steps it becomes $n$ kernels multiplied and $n-1$ nested integrals.

$$p(x_k)=\int \underbrace{\pi(x_k\mid x')}_{\text{How you move}}\;\underbrace{p_{k-1 \mid k-1}(x')}_{\text{where you were}}\;dx'$$
In words: *you don't know where you were, so consider every possible previous state $x'$, **weight** it by how plausible it was, and from each one push forward by the dynamics.* That's just the law of total probability with a continuous "where you were." **Chapman–Kolmogorov** is the name it gets when the thing being marginalized out is an intermediate time in a Markov chain.

**The anchor.** On a discrete state space this is plain matrix multiplication,

$$P^{(m+n)}=P^{(m)}P^{(n)},\qquad \big[P^{(m)}P^{(n)}\big]_{ij}=\sum_{l}P^{(m)}_{il}P^{(n)}_{lj}$$

with $l$ the intermediate state. The integral version is the same statement with $l$ ranging over $\mathbb{R}^{n_x}$ instead of a finite index set. Closer to home still: it is the density-level analogue of the semigroup property of the state transition matrix,

$$\Phi(t_2,t_0)=\Phi(t_2,t_1)\,\Phi(t_1,t_0)$$

Deterministically, maps on *points* compose by matrix product. Stochastically, kernels on *densities* compose by an integral. Nothing else changed.

**The reading that removes the mystique.** The integral is a marginalisation of a joint:

$$p(x_k,x'\mid z_{1:k-1})=\pi(x_k\mid x')\,p(x'\mid z_{1:k-1}) \quad\longrightarrow\quad \int (\cdot)\,dx'$$

Build the joint over (old state, new state), then discard the old state. That is all the $\int \cdot\, dx'$ does.

**Why the filter never computes the $n$-step product.** In the recursion only *one* kernel and *one* integral appear. Every earlier path factor is already compressed into $p_{k-1|k-1}$ — that is precisely what the Markov assumption buys, and it is why the filter costs $O(1)$ per step rather than growing with $k$. The nested multi-step form is what a non-Markov process would force.

**Continuous time.** The same identity survives; taking the one-step limit $\Delta t\to 0$ turns it into the Kolmogorov forward (Fokker–Planck) PDE. Two separate axes are involved and should not be conflated: *state space* discrete vs continuous changes $\sum\leftrightarrow\int$ and nothing else; *time* discrete vs continuous changes an algebraic identity into a differential one. See 2020-Garcia-Fernandez-ContinuousDiscreteMultiple for the continuous-discrete filter that this matters for.

#### The prediction step is CK, evaluated

Linear-Gaussian case: $$p_{k-1|k-1}=\mathcal{N}(x';\hat x_{k-1|k-1},P_{k-1|k-1})$$ and $$\pi(x_k\mid x')=\mathcal{N}(x_k;Fx',Q),$$i.e. $x_k=Fx'+v$ with $v\sim\mathcal{N}(0,Q)$ independent of $x'$.

No algebra is required, only two closure properties:

1. a linear ($F$ is linear function) map of a Gaussian is Gaussian: $Fx'\sim\mathcal{N}(F\hat x,FPF^\top)$
2. a $\sum$ of independent Gaussians is Gaussian: adding $v$ adds $Q$

$$\boxed{\;\int \mathcal{N}(x_k;Fx',Q)\,\mathcal{N}(x';\hat x,P)\,dx'=\mathcal{N}\big(x_k;\;F\hat x,\;FPF^\top+Q\big)\;}$$
which is literally
$$\hat{x}_{k|k-1}=F\hat{x}_{k-1|k-1},\qquad P_{k|k-1}=FP_{k-1|k-1}F^\top+Q$$

**The two Kalman prediction lines are the Chapman–Kolmogorov integral.** They appear as two lines rather than an integral only because the Gaussian family is closed under this operation, so the entire ***infinite-dimensional object is carried by two moments***. Every approximate filter in the list above exists because that closure fails somewhere: 

- nonlinear $f_k$ (EKF, UKF),
	- Property 1 dies — $f(x')$ of a Gaussian is not Gaussian if $f(\cdot)$ is a non-linear function, so the pushforward leaves the family immediately.
- non-Gaussian $p_v$ (particle), 
	- The sum of two independent non-Gaussians is some other density with skew, fat tails, whatever — and those two moments no longer pin it down. You can still compute them; they just stop describing the thing. And the moment you hit the Bayes update with a non-Gaussian likelihood, the two numbers you carried can't reconstruct the posterior.
- multi-modal posterior (Gaussian sum).
	- the posterior has a **finite, fixed-size sufficient statistic**. Gaussian is just the family where that size is 2 and never grows. The Gaussian sum family is also closed, but its statistic grows as $2\cdot3^n$, which is the same as not having one.

#### Bayes’ rule

The Chapman Kolmogorov equation propagates without data whereas the Bayes conditions without dynamics. The filtering is nothing more then alternating between the two: **Prediction/Update**. Bayes multiplies densities pointwise, so when multiplying gaussians there is necessity for a third **closure property**.

**The two are different operations that merely look alike on the page.** Both contain an integral, and that is the entire resemblance:

| | Chapman–Kolmogorov | Bayes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Driven by | the **model** (dynamics) | the **data** (a measurement) |
| Formula | $p(x_k)=\int \pi(x_k\mid x')\,p(x')\,dx'$ | $p(x_k\mid z_k)=\dfrac{g(z_k\mid x_k)\,p(x_k)}{\int g(z_k\mid x)p(x)dx}$ |
| Acts on the density by | **convolution** — smears mass around | **pointwise multiplication**, then renormalisation |
| Effect on uncertainty | grows (adds $Q$) | shrinks (removes possibilities) |
| What the integral is over | the *previous* state $x'$ | the *current* state, and only to normalise |

The last row is the load-bearing one. In CK the integral **is** the work: a variable is marginalised out and the output is a new *function* of $x_k$. In Bayes the numerator already is the answer up to scale — the denominator is a single number, the evidence $\mathcal{L}(z_k)$ derived below. Bayes moves no mass; it only decides which parts of the existing density to keep believing in.

**Why only the exponent is ever looked at:**
A Gaussian is: $$\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}}\exp(-\frac{(x-a)^2}{2\sigma^2}).$$The pre-factor does not contain $x$, and Bayes renormalizes at the end anyway, so every $x$-free factor is discarded — write $\propto$ and drop it. What remains is the exponent, and multiplying exponentials *adds* exponents. 

**Multiplying two Gaussians is adding two quadratics in $x$.** That is the whole mechanism; the matrix version in the next section is this sentence with $\top$ signs on it.

**The third closure property.** Take the scalar case and collect only the $x^2$ terms:

$$\mathcal{N}(x;a,\sigma_1^2)\,\mathcal{N}(x;b,\sigma_2^2)\;\propto\;\exp\!\left(-\tfrac12\underbrace{\Big(\tfrac{1}{\sigma_1^2}+\tfrac{1}{\sigma_2^2}\Big)}_{=\,1/\sigma^{2}}x^2+(\text{linear})+(\text{const})\right)$$

A quadratic in the **exponent *is* the definition of Gaussian shape**, so the family is closed — this is **conjugacy** (Bayesian Statistics). 
Matching against $$-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}$$ reads off the variance immediately:

$$\boxed{\;\frac{1}{\sigma^{2}}=\frac{1}{\sigma_1^{2}}+\frac{1}{\sigma_2^{2}}\;}$$

$1/\sigma^2$ is the **precision**. So the duality that carries the whole note:

- **CK / convolution → variances add.** Uncertainty grows.
- **Bayes / multiplication → precisions add.** Certainty grows.

Precisions adding forces $\sigma^2$ below *both* $\sigma_1^2$ and $\sigma_2^2$: combining two independent pieces of evidence always beats either alone. This is the scalar shadow of **information adds** in the information form below.

**The mean.** Matching the linear term the same way:

$$\mu=\frac{\dfrac{a}{\sigma_1^2}+\dfrac{b}{\sigma_2^2}}{\dfrac{1}{\sigma_1^2}+\dfrac{1}{\sigma_2^2}}$$

A **precision-weighted average** — whichever source is more certain pulls harder. Check: $\sigma_2^2\to\infty$ sends its weight to zero and $\mu\to a$, i.e. a useless measurement does not move the estimate.

**The gain, scalar.** Set $$a=\hat x^-, \sigma_1^2=P^-$$ and — with $H=1$ — read the likelihood $g(z\mid x)$ at fixed $z$ as Gaussian *in $x$*, centered at $z$ with variance $R$, so $$b=z,\ \sigma_2^2=R$$. Then $$P^+=\big(\tfrac{1}{P^-}+\tfrac1R\big)^{-1}$$ and $$\mu=\frac{R\hat x^-+P^-z}{P^-+R}$$. Defining $K=\dfrac{P^-}{P^-+R}:\dfrac{R}{P^-+R}=1-K$ and rearranging:

$$\hat x^+=\hat x^-+K\,(z-\hat x^-),\qquad P^+=(1-K)\,P^-$$

**The Kalman gain is the fraction of total precision contributed by the measurement.** $R$ small $\Rightarrow K\to1$, jump to $z$; $P^-$ small $\Rightarrow K\to0$, ignore $z$. Nothing was designed — it fell out of a product of two exponentials.

The next section repeats exactly this with vectors, where $\tfrac{1}{\sigma^2}\mapsto(P^-)^{-1}$, $\tfrac{1}{R}\mapsto H^\top R^{-1}H$, and the rearrangement into gain form needs the matrix inversion lemma instead of arithmetic.

## Unnormalized posterior

Write the prior shorthand: 
- $\hat x^-\equiv\hat x_{k|k-1}$, 
- $P^-\equiv P_{k|k-1}$, 
- $g(z\mid x)=\mathcal{N}(z;Hx,R)$. 

Per framework step 4, keep it unnormalized:
$$q(x)\;=\;g(z_k\mid x)\,p_{k|k-1}(x)\;=\;\mathcal{N}(z_k;Hx,R)\,\mathcal{N}(x;\hat x^-,P^-)$$
Both factors are exponentials of quadratics, so collect the exponent in $x$:
$$-\tfrac{1}{2}\Big[(z_k-Hx)^\top R^{-1}(z_k-Hx)+(x-\hat x^-)^\top (P^-)^{-1}(x-\hat x^-)\Big]$$
$$=-\tfrac{1}{2}\Big[x^\top\underbrace{\big((P^-)^{-1}+H^\top R^{-1}H\big)}_{\text{posterior precision}}x-2x^\top\underbrace{\big((P^-)^{-1}\hat x^- + H^\top R^{-1}z_k\big)}_{\text{posterior information vector}}\Big]+\text{const}(x)$$

So the posterior is Gaussian with, in **information form**,

$$(P^+)^{-1}=(P^-)^{-1}+H^\top R^{-1}H,\qquad (P^+)^{-1}\hat x^+=(P^-)^{-1}\hat x^-+H^\top R^{-1}z_k$$
Read directly: **information adds.** The prior contributes $(P^-)^{-1}$, the measurement contributes $H^\top R^{-1}H$, and the posterior carries the sum. That is the Bayes update with all the notation removed.

### Recovering the gain form

Apply the matrix inversion lemma to the precision:

$$P^+=\big((P^-)^{-1}+H^\top R^{-1}H\big)^{-1}=P^- - P^-H^\top\underbrace{\big(HP^-H^\top+R\big)}_{\textstyle S}{}^{-1}HP^-$$
Define $K=P^-H^\top S^{-1}$, so $P^+=(I-KH)P^-$. For the mean, use the companion identity $\big((P^-)^{-1}+H^\top R^{-1}H\big)^{-1}H^\top R^{-1}=P^-H^\top S^{-1}=K$:
$$\hat x^+=P^+\big((P^-)^{-1}\hat x^-+H^\top R^{-1}z_k\big)=(I-KH)\hat x^-+Kz_k=\hat x^-+K\big(z_k-H\hat x^-\big)$$

The gain equation falls out of the product $g(z\mid x)p(x)$ and one matrix identity. The gain is not a separate design object — it is the change of variables that turns "information adds" into "correct the mean by a weighted innovation".

## Normaliser — and what it becomes one level up

$$\mathcal{L}(z_k)\;=\;\int g_k(z_k\mid x)\,p_{k|k-1}(x)\,dx$$

Evaluate it the same way as the prediction integral. Since $z_k=Hx+w$ with $x\sim\mathcal{N}(\hat x^-,P^-)$ and $w\sim\mathcal{N}(0,R)$ independent:

$$\boxed{\;\mathcal{L}(z_k)=\mathcal{N}\big(z_k;\;\underbrace{H\hat x^-}_{\hat z},\;\underbrace{HP^-H^\top+R}_{S}\big)\;}$$

**The frightening integral is the predicted measurement distribution, evaluated at the measurement actually received.** $\hat z$ and $S$ are already computed for the gain — $K=P^-H^\top S^{-1}$ — so this quantity has been present in every Kalman implementation, just never named.

Equivalently, the whole update is one exact factorisation:

$$\underbrace{\mathcal{N}(z;Hx,R)\,\mathcal{N}(x;\hat x^-,P^-)}_{\text{unnormalised } q(x)}=\underbrace{\mathcal{N}(z;\hat z,S)}_{\text{evidence, no }x}\cdot\underbrace{\mathcal{N}(x;\hat x^+,P^+)}_{\text{posterior}}$$

Prior × likelihood splits cleanly into (a number scoring the measurement) × (the updated density). Framework step 5 asks what the denominator becomes one level up — here is the answer, in three places:

- **Gating.** $\ln\mathcal{L}=-\tfrac12\big[d^\top S^{-1}d+\ln|2\pi S|\big]$ with innovation $d=z-\hat z$. The Mahalanobis distance $d^\top S^{-1}d$ is $\chi^2$ with $n_z$ degrees of freedom under the correct-association hypothesis. Gating is thresholding this log-evidence, nothing more.
- **Bernoulli (A2).** The detection branch carries weight $r\,p_D\,\mathcal{L}(z)$ against the misdetection branch $r(1-p_D)$ and the clutter explanation $\lambda_{\text{FA}}(z)$. $\mathcal{L}$ is the only term in that competition that depends on *where* the measurement landed.
- **PDA / JPDA (A4, A5).** With $m$ measurements, $\mathcal{L}_i=\mathcal{N}(z_i;\hat z,S)$ per measurement, and the association weights are $\beta_i\propto p_D\mathcal{L}_i$ against $\beta_0\propto\lambda_{\text{FA}}(1-p_D)$-type terms. The per-hypothesis update inside each branch is unchanged — it is exactly the $\hat x^+$, $P^+$ above.

The same scalar plays all three roles. That is the thread Derivation framework is pointing at when it says the per-hypothesis mathematics is machinery already owned.

## Result (Kalman filter update)

$$\hat x_{k|k-1}=F\hat x_{k-1|k-1},\qquad P_{k|k-1}=FP_{k-1|k-1}F^\top+Q \tag{CK}$$
**Predicted Measurement Covariance:**
$$S_k=HP_{k|k-1}H^\top+R,$$
**Kalman Gain:**
$$K_k=P_{k|k-1}H^\top S_k^{-1} $$
**Posterior Mean:**$$\hat x_{k|k}=\hat x_{k|k-1}+K_k\big(z_k-H\hat x_{k|k-1}\big),$$
**Posterior Covariance:**
$$P_{k|k}=(I-K_kH)P_{k|k-1}$$
**Posterior Measurement Density:**
$$\mathcal{L}(z_k)=\mathcal{N}\big(z_k;H\hat x_{k|k-1},S_k\big) \tag{evidence}$$

Line 1 is Chapman–Kolmogorov evaluated in closed form. Lines 2–3 are $g\cdot p$ normalised. Line 4 is the normaliser that classical presentations discard and every multi-object filter needs.

## Extreme checks

- **$R\to 0$ (perfect sensor), $H$ square and invertible:** $K\to H^{-1}$, so $\hat x^+=H^{-1}z$ and $P^+\to 0$. The prior is discarded entirely. Note $P^+=0$ is an **absorbing state** — Cromwell's rule in continuous form: zero covariance means zero information capacity, and no later measurement can ever move the estimate. Same failure mode as $r=1$ in A2.
- **$R\to\infty$ (useless sensor):** $K\to 0$, $\hat x^+=\hat x^-$, $P^+=P^-$. The measurement is ignored. Simultaneously $S\to\infty$ so $\mathcal{L}$ flattens towards zero and stops discriminating between measurement locations — an uninformative sensor cannot do data association either, which is the A4/A5 statement of the same limit.
- **$P^-\to\infty$ (no prior):** the information form gives $(P^+)^{-1}=H^\top R^{-1}H$ and $\hat x^+=(H^\top R^{-1}H)^{-1}H^\top R^{-1}z$ — weighted least squares. Bayes with a flat prior *is* the deterministic estimator from the control courses.
- **$H$ not full column rank ($n_z<n_x$, partial observation):** $H^\top R^{-1}H$ is singular, so the precision only increases on the observed subspace. The unobserved directions keep exactly their prior uncertainty and are corrected only through $F$ in the next prediction. This is observability, written in covariance instead of rank.
- **$Q\to 0$, $F=I$ (static object, repeated looks):** $(P_{k|k})^{-1}=(P_0)^{-1}+k\,H^\top R^{-1}H$, so uncertainty falls as $1/k$ — the recursive form of averaging $k$ measurements. Directly relevant to the thesis: crops do not move, so this is the regime the mapping half of the problem actually sits in.
- **$p_D\to 1$, $\lambda_{\text{FA}}\to 0$, $r\to 1$:** the branch table collapses to one row and A2/A4/A5 all reduce to this note. Confirms this derivation is the base case the rest re-inflates from.

## What I could not derive
- The matrix inversion lemma and the companion identity $\big((P^-)^{-1}+H^\top R^{-1}H\big)^{-1}H^\top R^{-1}=P^-H^\top S^{-1}$ are used here as stated results, not proved. Mechanical, but currently borrowed.
- Optimality is not established. This note shows the Gaussian family is *closed* under CK + Bayes; it does not show the Kalman estimator is MMSE, nor that it is the best *linear* estimator when the noise is non-Gaussian. That second claim is the one worth being able to defend at a meeting.
- The normaliser is derived here for **one** branch. How competing normalisers are compared, normalised against each other and pruned across *global* hypotheses is open — this is **A3**, and it is the same hole §7 of A2 Bernoulli existence update flags.
- Continuous-time: the passage from CK to Fokker–Planck is asserted, not taken. Only needed if the continuous-discrete formulation of 2020-Garcia-Fernandez-ContinuousDiscreteMultiple enters the scope.
